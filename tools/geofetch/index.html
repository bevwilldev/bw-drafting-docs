GeoFetch

Pulls published government spatial data straight into the drawing — cadastre, addresses, roads, zoning, planning overlays, hazards, utilities and terrain — for NSW, Victoria, Queensland and Auckland.

GeoFetch was written by Phuong Luong and is distributed with the suite. It installs with the Engineering workstation and with the full suite.

Opening it

Run GEOFETCH, or use the button on the WSY Tools ribbon tab. That opens the manager, where you choose the region. Each region can also be opened directly: NSWFETCH, AKLFETCH, VICFETCH, QLDFETCH.

How a fetch works

Every region's dialog asks the same three things before anything else:

  1. Where — type a centre easting and northing, or click Pick < and pick it in the drawing.
  2. How much — a window width and height in metres. Up to 2 000 m for most data; 1 000 m where terrain is involved.
  3. What — tick the datasets you want.

Then Fetch. Everything arrives on layers prefixed for the region (GEO- for NSW, VIC-, and so on), as ordinary polylines, text, hatches and points — nothing custom, so the drawing looks identical on a machine without the plugins.

Note — a fetch holds BricsCAD until it finishes, and Esc will not interrupt it. A large multi-dataset fetch can take minutes and looks like a freeze. It isn't one, and it always comes back — don't kill BricsCAD waiting.

Settings you can keep

Any combination of datasets and options can be saved as a named profile and recalled next time, which is worth doing for the sets you fetch repeatedly.

Elevation data — read this before using it on a job

The terrain options are context, not survey. Use them for extent, fall, catchment sense and early feasibility. Nothing produced from them should carry through to design levels or certified output without a real survey surface underneath.

NSW offers three elevation options and they are not equivalent:

OptionWhat it actually is
Contours (2m) Pre-drawn contour linework from the NSW 1:25 000 topographic series — captured around the year 2000, stated vertical accuracy ±5 m. Background linework.
from DEM Contours generated in the drawing from the 5 m elevation raster, at whatever interval you type.
DEM → TIN A BricsCAD surface built from the same 5 m raster, at a grid spacing of 5 m or coarser.

Three things worth knowing

  • The source is 5 m data, and the tool will happily draw finer than that. Grid spacing is floored at 5 m because you can't sample finer than the source — but the contour interval has no such limit. Ask for 1 m contours and you'll get them; they will look three times more precise than the data behind them. Ask for 0.25 m and they'll look twenty times more precise.
  • A generated surface looks exactly like a surveyed one. A TinSurface from GeoFetch is visually indistinguishable in BricsCAD from one built off real pickups. Anyone downstream who didn't watch you make it has no way to tell.
  • The coverage is a mosaic. NSW's 5 m elevation data was captured over many years by more than one method, varying by area — and the tool doesn't record which applied to your fetch, so the drawing can't answer that question later. Treat the terrain as unattributed unless someone checks the source coverage for that specific area.

Auckland is different — its contours come from the council's 2024 LiDAR survey and the dialog labels them so. They are a genuinely better product than the NSW terrain options, though the same rule applies about design levels.

What else is indicative

Some datasets are marked indicative at the source and are drawn that way:

  • Utilities (water, sewer, stormwater) — approximate positions for context. A Before You Dig Australia enquiry is still mandatory before any excavation, and beforeUdig in New Zealand.
  • Easements — the cadastral easement layer is incomplete statewide. Always verify against the title and the plan.

Attribution

Every fetch stamps the drawing with the data source, the licence, the date fetched, the datum and the words "Indicative only — verify by survey." The stamp is not optional: attribution is a condition of the licences the data is published under, and it is also the only thing telling a later reader where the linework came from. Leave it in the drawing.

Keeping data current, and removing it

NSWFETCHUPDATE (and the Auckland, Victoria and Queensland equivalents) re-fetches what the drawing already holds, so the data matches what the agency publishes today. NSWFETCHDELETE and its equivalents remove it again. Both work from a record GeoFetch keeps in the drawing, so they only touch what GeoFetch drew.

Note — update is all-or-nothing: it refreshes every dataset in the record, not just the stale one, so it costs a full fetch.

The cache folder

Each fetch writes a geofetch_cache folder beside the drawing, holding the raw responses and a manifest of what was fetched and when. It's the audit trail — how the drawing can be reconciled later against what the agency served on the day.

Nothing cleans it up, so on a project share these accumulate. They are safe to delete: the drawing keeps its geometry, and only the ability to reconcile or re-fetch from the cache is lost.

If it fails

GeoFetch needs internet access to the agency services. Transient failures are retried automatically, and the hosts are sometimes slow — hence the long waits. If a dataset can't be fetched completely, the tool says so and asks whether to keep or discard what it got, rather than leaving you with a partial layer that looks complete. That refusal is deliberate: half a cadastre that looks whole is worse than none.
